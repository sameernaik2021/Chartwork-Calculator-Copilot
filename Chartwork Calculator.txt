Chartwork Calculator
===================
A Chartwork Calculator is a tool—traditionally done manually with a nautical chart, dividers, parallel rulers, compass, and calculator, and now often assisted by software—that helps a navigator perform the mathematical calculations required for marine navigation.
In simple terms:
It takes information about a ship's position, route, speed, time, wind/current, etc., and helps determine where the ship should go, how long it will take, and how environmental forces will affect its movement.
It is particularly useful when studying Nautical Science, navigation, and deck-officer subjects.

1. Distance
A chartwork calculator can determine the distance between two positions.
For example:
Position A ───────────────────────► Position B
              120 nautical miles
If the ship needs to travel from A to B and the distance is 120 nautical miles, that distance can be used for ETA calculations.
Important unit
Marine navigation normally uses the nautical mile (NM).
1 nautical mile = 1 minute of latitude approximately.

2. Course
A course is the direction in which the ship is intended to travel.
Courses are normally expressed in degrees from north:
    • 000° → North
    • 090° → East
    • 180° → South
    • 270° → West
Suppose the calculated route from Point A to Point B has a direction of:
075°
The navigator can use this information when planning the passage.
However, nautical calculations distinguish between different types of direction, such as:
    • True course
    • Magnetic course
    • Compass course
    • Course over ground
Understanding the difference is important.

3. Speed
Ship speed is normally expressed in knots.
What is a knot?
1 knot = 1 nautical mile per hour.
So if a ship travels at:
12 knots
it theoretically travels:
12 nautical miles in one hour.

4. Distance–Speed–Time Calculation
One of the fundamental calculations in navigation is:
Time=DistanceSpeedTime = \frac{Distance}{Speed}
For example:
Distance = 120 NM
Speed = 12 knots
Therefore:
Time=12012=10 hoursTime = \frac{120}{12}=10\text{ hours}
So the ship would require approximately 10 hours, assuming the speed remains constant and ignoring other effects.
Similarly:
Distance=Speed×TimeDistance = Speed \times Time
and:
Speed=DistanceTimeSpeed = \frac{Distance}{Time}
These three relationships are fundamental to chartwork.

5. ETA — Estimated Time of Arrival
ETA means Estimated Time of Arrival.
A chartwork calculator can help determine when a ship should reach a particular waypoint or destination.
Suppose:
    • Departure: 08:00
    • Distance: 180 NM
    • Speed: 15 knots
Then:
Time=18015=12 hoursTime = \frac{180}{15}=12\text{ hours}
Therefore:
ETA ≈ 20:00
This is a simplified calculation. Real voyage planning can require adjustments for currents, weather, traffic, speed changes, and other factors.

6. Set and Drift
This is one of the most important concepts in nautical navigation.
Imagine that your ship is trying to travel north.
                  Intended track
                       ↑
                       │
                       │
                       │
                       │
                       │
                  Ship's route
But there is an ocean current moving toward the east.
Current
──────────────►
The current pushes the ship sideways.
Therefore, although the ship is moving through the water in one direction, its actual movement over the Earth's surface may be different.
This is called set and drift.
Set
Set = the direction in which the current carries the vessel.
Drift
Drift = the speed at which the current carries the vessel.
For example:
Current:
    • Set = 090°
    • Drift = 2 knots
This means the current is carrying the vessel toward 090° (east) at 2 knots.

7. Course Through Water vs Course Over Ground
This distinction is extremely important.
Course Through Water
The direction in which the vessel moves relative to the surrounding water.
Course Over Ground (COG)
The actual direction in which the vessel moves relative to the Earth's surface.
If there is a current:

A chartwork calculator can help determine the relationship between:
Ship's velocity + Current velocity = Resultant velocity
This is essentially a vector problem.

The navigator wants to know what course to steer to achieve a desired track.

9. Course to Steer
Suppose you want your ship to follow a particular track, but a current is pushing the ship sideways.
You cannot necessarily steer directly toward the destination.
Instead, you may need to steer into the current.

This is called current correction or tidal/current correction, depending on the situation.

10. Dead Reckoning (DR)
Chartwork calculators are also useful for dead reckoning.
Dead reckoning estimates the vessel's position using information such as:
    • Previous known position
    • Course
    • Speed
    • Time
For example:
A ship starts at:
Position A
and travels:
090° at 10 knots for 3 hours
Distance traveled:
10×3=30 NM10 \times 3 = 30\text{ NM}
The navigator can plot the resulting position on the chart.
This produces a DR position.

11. Estimated Position (EP)
An Estimated Position (EP) can incorporate additional information, such as the effects of current.
Conceptually:
DR position
     ●
      \
       \
        ● EP
The difference between the DR position and the estimated position can result from environmental effects such as current.

12. Fixing the Ship's Position
A navigator may also need to determine the vessel's actual position using observations.
Traditional methods include:
    • Bearings of landmarks
    • Astronomical observations
    • Radar ranges/bearings
    • Visual observations
Modern navigation may also use:
    • GNSS/GPS
    • Radar
    • ECDIS
    • AIS and other navigation information
Chartwork remains important because navigators need to understand how position determination works, not merely read a GPS display.

13. Bearings
A bearing describes the direction of an object from the vessel.
For example:
Lighthouse bearing = 045°
This means the lighthouse is approximately northeast of the vessel.
Multiple bearings can be used to establish a position.
For example:
        Lighthouse
             ●
            /
           /
          /
         ● Ship
          \
           \
            \
             ● Island
The navigator can use bearings from known objects to determine the ship's position on the chart.

14. Course, Bearing, and Heading Are Not the Same
These terms are often confused by beginners.
Heading
The direction the ship's bow is pointing.
Course
The intended direction of travel.
Bearing
The direction of an object relative to the ship or a reference direction.
Course Over Ground
The actual direction the vessel is moving over the Earth's surface.
A chartwork calculator can help relate these different quantities.

15. ETA With Current
Suppose:
    • Distance = 240 NM
    • Ship speed = 12 knots
Without current:
Time=24012=20 hoursTime = \frac{240}{12}=20\text{ hours}
But if environmental conditions cause the vessel's actual speed over ground to become, for example, 10 knots, then:
Time=24010=24 hoursTime = \frac{240}{10}=24\text{ hours}
So the ETA changes significantly.
This is why navigation calculations cannot always be based simply on the engine's stated speed.

16. Why Chartwork Calculators Are Important in Nautical Science
Learning chartwork develops the ability to answer practical navigation questions such as:
Where am I?
Where am I going?
Which course should I steer?
How far do I have to travel?
How long will it take?
What effect will the current have?
Where will I actually be after several hours?
When should I expect to arrive?
These are fundamental questions for a navigating officer.

18. Example: Complete Simple Problem
Suppose:
Starting position: Point A
Destination: Point B
Distance: 150 NM
Ship speed: 15 knots
Step 1 — Calculate time
Time=15015=10 hoursTime=\frac{150}{15}=10\text{ hours}
Step 2 — Departure
Suppose departure is:
06:00
Step 3 — Calculate ETA
10 hours after 06:00:
ETA = 16:00
Now suppose a current reduces the effective speed over ground to 12 knots.
Then:
Time=15012=12.5 hoursTime=\frac{150}{12}=12.5\text{ hours}
Therefore:
ETA ≈ 18:30
This simple example demonstrates why environmental conditions matter.

19. Traditional Chartwork vs Software
There are two important approaches.
Traditional chartwork
Uses:
    • Paper nautical charts
    • Dividers
    • Parallel rulers
    • Compass
    • Protractor
    • Nautical almanac
    • Calculator
Electronic navigation
Uses systems such as:
    • ECDIS
    • GNSS
    • Radar
    • Electronic plotting
    • Integrated bridge systems
A chartwork calculator can sit between these worlds: it teaches the mathematical principles behind navigation and can also automate calculations.

20. What You Should Learn First
If you're studying Nautical Science, don't start by memorizing calculator buttons.
Learn in this order:
    1. Latitude and longitude
    2. Nautical miles
    3. Compass directions and degrees
    4. Course and bearing
    5. Speed, distance and time
    6. Dead reckoning
    7. Position fixing
    8. True, magnetic and compass directions
    9. Variation and deviation
    10. Set and drift
    11. Course to steer
    12. ETA calculations
    13. Tides and tidal streams
    14. Chart symbols and publications
    15. Electronic navigation systems
Once these concepts are clear, a chartwork calculator becomes much easier to use.
The key idea
Think of a chartwork calculator as a navigation mathematics assistant:
Chart + Position + Course + Speed + Time + Current → Calculated route, position, ETA, and course to steer.
And importantly, the calculator is not the navigator. A deck officer needs to understand the underlying navigation principles so that the results can be interpreted, checked, and used safely.
